Learner brief for Week 1

What you will be able to do after this week

  • Write the problem in your current work as one sentence, with a named person who bears the consequence and a “done when…” test.
  • Choose one candidate number for the ship item, and say where it is measured and who sees it change. This number is not final; you confirm or change it in week 2.
  • Break a problem down to what is certain by yourself, using Five Whys and the six types of Socratic question, and separate it from what is only an assumption.
  • Check that your ship item is small enough for the build to fit in about 8 hours in total across week 3 and week 4 (a provisional number, to be adjusted after the first course run), and that you can release it yourself without waiting for the company’s release calendar.

What to bring to Session 1

  • The two candidate problems from week 0 (who is harmed, how many times a week, three times “why”).
  • Paper and a pen, or the note-taking tool you are comfortable with. The first part of the session uses no AI.
  • A laptop with an AI tool of your choice, and access to the place where the numbers for your chosen problem come from (log, spreadsheet, ticket system) if there is one.

How you are graded

In Session 2, each piece of work is graded by two other learners in a rotation; you do not grade your own work. In class there are 5 “hot seat” turns of 10 minutes each; in your turn, you re-measure the number live for 4 minutes, then answer Socratic questions for 3 minutes, while the two graders record their results separately, without discussing them with each other. The graders look only at the record, the screenshots and the live re-measure. A spoken account does not count. They answer four questions:

  1. Is there a record, a screenshot or a live run, or only a spoken account?
  2. Can it be linked to the number of the ship item?
  3. Can you answer the Socratic questions about your own decisions?
  4. Does the submission clearly separate what is certain from what is only an assumption?

The result is one of three: pass, needs revision, not enough evidence. The graders write the reason. If the two graders differ, the stricter result is kept; the facilitator does not decide for them. To pass, this week needs: the problem packed into one sentence, an observable test, a candidate number with a way to measure it, and a named person who bears the consequence. You revise and submit again; the revised version is evidence that feedback worked. Each person also has 5 minutes to defend one decision out loud, with no AI open.

If you are stuck

  • You cannot choose a problem yet: use the second problem from your week 0 task.
  • Your framing sentence keeps mentioning a tool: remove the tool and rewrite it using the template “[role] loses [time, money or errors] every [how often] because of [task]; done when [test]”.
  • You have no data to measure: count by hand for one week, or write “not measured” and find out who holds the data.
  • Your manager has not replied: send one more short message; if there is still no reply, tell the facilitator in Session 2.
  • You are still stuck after 30 minutes: write down exactly where you are stuck and bring it to Session 2. The unblocking support is for this.

First Principles Loop

A first principle is a fact that cannot be broken down further. The method of the course has five steps. In the part above you ran steps 1 to 4 by hand. Step 5 is done in the ship item sheet.

  1. Frame. One sentence stating the problem, with an observable success test.
  2. Break down. Ask “why” repeatedly (Five Whys) and use the six types of Socratic question, stating clearly what is certain and what is only an assumption.
  3. Ask what would make this unnecessary. If there is an answer, the problem has changed.
  4. Rebuild. Build the solution only from what is certain, then invert it to see how it fails.
  5. Predict, then test. Write the prediction first, then run the cheapest test that could disprove it.

From now on, every submission opens with a list of what is certain, kept apart from assumptions.

Four exercises by role

Each exercise has the same structure: weak frame, five “why” questions, what is certain and assumption, the unnecessity question, right frame. Do the exercise for your own role first, then read the other three to see the same loop running in another role. Write your own five “why” questions before you open the model answer.

Dev: “Wrap retry around the payment command”

The owner’s example. The chain of “why” was built by the author for teaching, following the owner’s conclusion (the real cause is a connection pool leak); the owner did not write a chain of five questions. Five Whys direction: downward, towards the cause.

Show the model answer
  • Weak frame: “Add retry to the payment command.”
  • Why 1: Why is retry needed? Because the command sometimes fails and the user sees an error.
  • Why 2: Why does the command fail? This is where you must stop and ask: have we looked at a real error log yet? If not, this answer is an assumption.
  • Why 3 (after looking at the log, following the owner’s conclusion): the error happens when the connection pool is exhausted.
  • Why 4: Why is it exhausted? Connections leak and are not returned.
  • Why 5: Why do they leak? The place that closes the connection is missing in one error-handling branch (an assumption built for the exercise, to be checked in the code).
  • What is certain: money moving out is a one-way door. Assumption: the error is caused by the network.
  • The unnecessity question: if the pool did not leak, would retry still be needed?
  • Right frame: an intermittent error reaches the user; done when the user-side error rate is close to 0 and there is no duplicate charge.
  • What to take away: retry wrapped around a cause that is not understood can double the consequence.

QA: “Automated tests are too slow”

Constructed example; the owner has no example yet for this concept in the QA role. The numbers in the example are constructed to illustrate, and are not real measurements. Five Whys direction: downward, towards the cause.

Show the model answer
  • Weak frame: “Speed up the automated test suite.”
  • Why 1: Why does it need to be faster? Because the test suite runs for 50 minutes so devs wait.
  • Why 2: Why do devs wait? Because they merge only after the test suite is green (a team rule, observed).
  • Why 3: Why must the whole suite run? Because it is not known which part this change touches (assumption).
  • Why 4: Why is it not known? Because tests are not linked to any feature.
  • Why 5: Why are they not linked? Because no one wrote down which criterion this test case protects.
  • What is certain: 50 minutes (if there is a record of the run), the rule of waiting for green. Assumption: devs really wait and do not switch to other work.
  • The unnecessity question: if merging did not need to wait for the whole suite, would this task still be worth doing?
  • Right frame: devs wait on average X minutes per merge; done when X falls to the threshold the team accepts, measured with the CI system’s record.
  • Candidate number: the time from opening a pull request to being able to merge. Person who bears the consequence: devs and reviewers.

Designer: “Customers do not finish signing up”

Constructed example; the owner has no example yet for this concept in the designer role. Five Whys direction: downward, towards the cause.

Show the model answer
  • Weak frame: “Redo the sign-up screen to look nicer.”
  • Why 1: Why redo it? Because the drop-off rate midway is high (needs a number; if there is none, it is an assumption).
  • Why 2: Why do they drop off? Hypothesis: the form is long. Probing assumptions question: have we seen at which step people drop off?
  • Why 3: Why that step? Assumption: it asks for the card number too early.
  • Why 4: Why must it ask early? Because the system needs to verify before creating the account (whether this constraint is real must be checked with the dev).
  • Why 5: Why is verification needed right away? Maybe not: the unnecessity question opens an option to do it later.
  • What is certain: only what is in the funnel data. Assumption: the reason for dropping off is the appearance.
  • Right frame: people who see the sign-up screen finish it at a rate of A%; done when that rate reaches B% in the same measurement window; measured with funnel events.
  • What to take away: “nicer” is not a test; a small change at one step can lie outside the screen (in the process).

Business direction: “Why does the company pay for this work”

Constructed example; the owner has no example yet of first principles applied to business. This exercise connects week 1 with week 2. Five Whys direction: upward, towards value. It uses the idea of “value created for whom” from the Value Proposition framework (Osterwalder, Pigneur, Bernarda, Smith, Value Proposition Design, Wiley, 2014): the customer has a job to be done, pains and desired gains. The details in the book have not been checked directly; the secondary summaries found match these three parts.

Constructed situation: a dev asks “I am about to write a tool that reconciles reports automatically for the accounting department. Why does the company pay for this work?”

Show the model answer
  • Why 1: Because accountants lose time reconciling by hand (person who bears the consequence: the accountant).
  • Why 2: Why is that time worth money? Because the closing report comes out late.
  • Why 3: Why does lateness matter? Because the head of department makes decisions late (assumption: not checked).
  • Why 4: Why do late decisions cost money? It needs a real example, otherwise this answer is an assumption.
  • Why 5: Why does the company choose to pay with your time and not buy software? The unnecessity question: is there a ready-made product?
  • What is certain: how many hours the accountant loses, if there is a record. Assumption: those hours really are moved to other valuable work.
  • Result: you may find that the candidate number is not “hours saved” but “days from the end of the period until the report is done”. The number is confirmed in week 2.
  • The owner’s related example: measuring with the wrong ruler (8-day growth article, Step 4). The owner has no example yet about price, channel or business model.
