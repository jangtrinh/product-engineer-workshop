Learner brief for Week 2

Status: draft, not yet run with learners. The hours are provisional and have no real measurement yet.

What you will be able to do

By the end of this week you can do three things. One: draw the chain from a business goal down to a technical decision in your ship item, and walk back up from a technical detail to the effect that people in the company will see. Two: settle the number for your ship item, either keep the candidate number from Week 1 or replace it with another number when the map shows it is not right, and start measuring the baseline window. Three: write a spec with acceptance criteria in Given, When, Then form, so that a stranger who reads it can conclude pass or fail.

The root question of the week: why does the company pay for this work, and if your change succeeds, what measurable thing does the company gain?

What you bring

  • Your ship item and candidate number from Week 1, together with the Week 1 peer-grading result.
  • The place where you can get the figures for that number: a dashboard, a report, a file, or a manual count. If the company does not let you take a screenshot, you need to be able to describe each step.
  • An AI tool of your choice. You use it after the break in Session 1, in the part where you apply the model to your ship item; before that, put it away.
  • Your page: paper and a pen, or any writing tool you are comfortable with. The first part of Session 1 is done on your page, with no AI.

Self-work this week

Estimated at 4 to 6 hours, guessed by the independent grader of the syllabus, with no measurement from any run. If you find that your real hours are very different, write them down so the facilitator can adjust.

  1. Finish the one-page spec: the problem in one sentence, in scope and out of scope, 3 to 5 acceptance criteria in Given, When, Then form. Each criterion has one line “how to check”. Anything you could not check is written as “not verified”.
  2. Settle the number and run the measurement table. Record the way of measuring, where the figures come from, the start date, the length of the baseline window and the reason for choosing that length, then record measurement points on a schedule you set yourself. There is no shared threshold; the length is your choice, and two other learners will judge it in the re-measure session.
  3. Read the learning material on value, unit economics, moat and runway (part D of this page) and fill in the combined exercise page at the end: the value proposition sentence, four lines of unit economics, three moat questions, one runway line. Label every number “measured” or “assumption”.
  4. Fix your work according to the peer-grading sheet after Session 2, then submit it again.
  5. Keep a log of your work with AI: each time you hand a task to AI, write what you handed over, what the AI returned, and how you checked it.

This week’s estimate does not include agent running cost, because you do not have an agent yet. In Week 4 you record the real agent running cost and convert it to one unit. The re-measure session compares two things separately: the value and the cost per unit against the Week 2 estimate, and the agent cost per unit against the figure recorded in Week 4. On the agent cost line you write: not estimated yet, measured in Week 4.

What you submit for the peer-grading session

Submit at least 24 hours before Session 2, so that your two graders can read it at home. One sheet or one page containing: the list of what is certain that your work rests on, kept apart from the list of what is still assumption; the two-way map; the sentence that settles the number; the spec; the measurement table page with a start date and at least one real measurement point; the combined exercise page at the end of the week’s learning material (value proposition sentence, unit economics, moat, runway); the AI log.

How you are graded

Each piece of work is graded by two other learners in a rotation; you do not grade your own work. In class there are 5 “hot seat” turns of 10 minutes: in your turn, you run one criterion live or re-measure one measurement point for 4 minutes, answer Socratic questions for 3 minutes, and then the two graders write down their results on their own, without discussing with each other. The graders look only at the record, the screenshots and the live run. Spoken accounts do not count. They answer four questions: is there real evidence; does it link to the number; can you answer the Socratic questions about your own decisions; does the work separate clearly what is certain from assumption.

This week you pass when each acceptance criterion can be checked and your number links to a business result that a specific person or report in the company pays attention to. The result is pass, needs revision, or not enough evidence, with a reason. You revise and submit again. If the two graders differ, the stricter result is kept. The peer-grading result decides the milestone of the week; the facilitator does not decide in its place; whether you complete the course is decided by the owner in the re-measure session.

In Session 2 you also have 5 minutes to defend one decision: whether you kept or replaced the number, why, which options you rejected, and what could make your conclusion wrong.

Permitted AI level

  • The first 20 minutes of Session 1 (break down to the root and rebuild it yourself): no AI.
  • Drawing the map, settling the number, writing the spec: AI may give you a draft for you to challenge. You point out what is wrong, fix it and defend your choice. AI does not decide in your place.
  • The 5-minute defence: no AI.

If you are stuck

  • No business number is accessible: think of who in the company would notice if this work changes, use the operating result they notice (waiting time, number of redos), write it as an assumption, and send your manager one question asking whether that number is tracked.
  • No historical data: count by hand within your window and state clearly that it is a manual count.
  • The acceptance criteria are still vague: give them to a colleague and ask whether they can conclude pass or fail without asking you.
  • The ship item looks too big: say so right away in Session 2. The facilitator will cut the scope with you; the quality stays the same.
  • Still stuck after 30 minutes: write down what you have tried and bring it to the unblocking support session. Being able to say where you are stuck is also a result.

Six types of Socratic question

While you work, the facilitator asks one question at a time, with no explanation. Sample questions for each type:

TypeSample question
Clarify“You wrote ‘more efficient’. Efficient by which ruler, and for whom?”
Probe assumptions“You are assuming your manager notices this number. How do you know that?”
Probe reasons and evidence“Where does this number live now: in which report, dashboard or meeting?”
Ask about consequences“If the number changes by 20% and nobody in the company notices, is your change still worth doing?”
Look for another viewpoint“A person in another department, such as finance or operations, what would they call this change?”
Question the question itself“Could it be that ‘what the company gains’ is not the right question? Who else could be the one who gains?”

The model in plain words

A change connects two ends. The business end is the result the company cares about. The technical end is the decision you make while doing the work. Between the two ends are two levels: the metric (which number measures that result) and how to re-measure (where to measure, for how long, compared with what).

Downward: from the goal to the metric, to the technical decision. Upward: from one technical detail up to the effect that someone in the company will see. If a detail cannot answer “who will see what”, it is not connected yet. A principle that goes with it: when time is short, cut the scope, not the quality.

1. Value proposition (value created for whom)

Definition

According to Osterwalder, Pigneur and Smith, a value proposition is designed to fit three things about the customer: the jobs they are trying to get done (customer jobs), the bad things they meet around that job (pains: bad feelings, costs, situations, risks), and the benefits they want (gains). The supply side states clearly which pain the product or service reduces and which gain it creates. When the two sides match, it is called “fit”. The source stresses ranking pains by how severe they are and how often they happen.

Why it matters for a product engineer

In Week 1 you named the person who bears the loss. This week you must say what that person receives, in that person’s own words. A change where nobody receives anything has no number to choose.

Apply to the work you are doing

The customer here is the person who uses the change: a colleague, another team, a manager, or an end user. Write one sentence in this pattern: “For [who], who has to [task] and suffers [pain], this change [reduces which pain or creates which gain], so they [result they notice themselves]”. The words “result they notice themselves” are where this connects to the number in Week 2.

Example

The owner has no example yet for this concept in the sense of the Osterwalder framework: the owner has not used the words jobs, pains, gains. The two nearest places only name who benefits and do not separate the job, the pain and the gain: the growth article says the goal is only that people who are doing design with AI can find the products, and that the products are all free (the owner’s 8-day growth article, section “Cách approach” [How we approach it]); the PO and AI design agent article records that the PO decided to deliver Figma first and the codebase after because the reviewer was waiting to see it, that is, the delivery order is set by the person who is waiting (the owner’s PO and AI design agent article, section “Ví dụ, bước 5” [Example, step five]).

Common mistakes

Writing the value for yourself (“I get less tired”) and calling it value. Sign: the sentence has no second person. Fix: ask “who besides me will notice this and can say it in their own words?”.

Exercise · 5 minutes

Write the value sentence for your ship item in the pattern above, on the combined exercise page. Underline the word for the recipient and the word for the result that person notices themselves. Give it to a colleague: can they say back who the recipient is?

Show the model answer

Constructed example (numbers are assumptions): a QA has a manual regression check before every release. The recipient is the release lead. Their job: decide whether to ship before 17:00 on Thursday. Pain: the test results arrive late, and sometimes the release has to be postponed. Gain they want: to know early and to know for sure. Value sentence: “For the release lead, who has to wait for the regression report until close to the cut-off time, this change runs an automated check suite for the payment flow and sends the report before 12:00, so they decide to ship without postponing because of waiting”.

2. Unit economics

Definition

Unit economics looks at profit and loss on one unit: one item sold or one customer, chosen by the analyst. For one item, the contribution margin equals the selling price of one unit minus the variable cost of that unit; fixed costs are not in the calculation, and the contribution margin is what remains to cover fixed costs and then create profit (Wikipedia, “Contribution margin”). For one customer, Skok defines CAC as the average cost of winning one new customer, LTV as the profit a typical customer creates while they stay, and the core question is whether LTV divided by CAC is large enough. Skok gives benchmarks for software as a service (SaaS): the best businesses have LTV divided by CAC above 3, and many recover CAC within 5–7 months; Skok treats these only as guidance, not as law.

Why it matters

A product engineer has to be able to answer the question the manager asks after every change: “is it worth it?”. The answer needs two numbers in the same unit: the value of one unit of work after the change, and the cost of that unit.

Apply to the work you are doing

Choose one unit of work that the change acts on: one release, one ticket, one report, one review session. Estimate three things for that unit. One, value: the time people save, the reduction in redos, or the result you chose on the two-way map, converted to the unit of measure the company uses. Two, variable cost: what costs extra each time the unit runs (hours of the person who maintains it, tool fees charged per use). Three, one-time fixed cost: the hours you spend to build the change. Divide the fixed cost by the contribution per unit to get the number of units needed to break even. If you do not have the company’s real money figures, use hours, and state clearly which numbers are assumptions. Leave the agent running cost empty: “not estimated yet, measured in Week 4”.

A condition that comes with Skok’s benchmarks: they are about the customers of a software company, and the author warns that whether capital is cheap or expensive changes what level is acceptable. Do not carry the benchmarks 3 or 5–7 months over to your change as a standard to pass.

Example

The cost part: the owner has one break-even calculation. In the Autonomous Design Agent article, JEV System One costs 42 USD for 1 billion tokens, 357 times cheaper than the Opus model (the article gives the Opus input price as about 15,000 USD for 1 billion tokens), so JEV may spend up to 357 tokens to save 1 Opus token and still break even; the final decision is still given to the high-tier model (the owner’s Autonomous Design Agent article, lines 545 and 713). The numbers are what the article states about itself; the writer has not re-run them. The value and revenue part: the owner has no example yet for this concept, because the owner’s products are free.

Common mistakes

One: converting everything to money with invented numbers to sound weighty. Sign: the number cannot be traced to any source. Fix: keep the unit as hours or counts, and write the assumption next to it. Two: leaving out maintenance cost and counting only build cost. Three: counting value on every unit when the change acts on only some of them. Four: measuring with the wrong ruler, as in the listing example in the growth article: judging a channel that creates reach by visits and then concluding it does not work (the owner’s 8-day growth article, section “Bước 4” [Step four]).

Exercise · 10 minutes

Choose a unit for your ship item. On the combined exercise page, fill in four lines: value per unit, variable cost per unit, one-time fixed cost, number of units to break even. Label each line “measured” or “assumption”. On the agent cost line write “not estimated yet, measured in Week 4”.

Show the model answer

Constructed example (numbers are assumptions, continuing the QA example in section 1). The unit: one release, 4 releases per month. Before: 6 hours of manual checking. After: 2 hours reviewing the automated report and approving exceptions, plus 0.5 hours maintaining the check suite. Assume the cost of one working hour is 150,000 dong (replace it with your own figure, or use hours only). Value per unit: 6 − 2 − 0.5 = 3.5 hours, about 525,000 dong. One-time fixed cost: 12 hours to build. Break-even after 12 divided by 3.5, about 3.4 releases, so round up to 4 releases, that is, one month. Not counted: how much the number of escaped defects falls, because that needs measuring. That line is written as “not measured”.

3. Moat (an advantage that is hard to copy)

Definition

Morningstar describes an economic moat as a durable competitive advantage that lets a business keep high returns on capital for many years, and lists five sources: switching costs, network effect, intangible assets, cost advantage, efficient scale. The page ties the moat to time: a wide moat is expected to last more than 20 years, a narrow moat about 10 years. Do not copy those year figures over to your work.

Why it matters

If anyone can redo an improvement in one afternoon, its value disappears when someone else redoes it, or when you change jobs. The moat question forces you to say what makes this benefit stay.

Apply to the work you are doing

Ask three questions. Is anyone else relying on this change to the point that removing it takes effort (switching costs)? Is there data or understanding that only you or your team have, and does the change use it (intangible assets)? Does it get better when more people use it (network effect)? If all three answers are no, your change has no moat, and you say so plainly and do not invent a moat.

Example

The owner’s OPC article puts a group of principles under the name “Chiến lược và moat” [Strategy and moat], which says that building a moat on purpose means switching cost, micro-network effects and one’s own micro-data, and also choosing a model that hurts the big players themselves if they copy it (the owner’s OPC article, section “Principles”, line 95). That is a summary of principles P3, P4 and P9; the writer has not opened the original Principles file, so there is no more detailed example from the owner.

Common mistakes

Calling every benefit a moat. Sign: you cannot answer “why can others not redo it, or not remove it?”. Fix: write down only a source that you can point to with one concrete thing.

Exercise · 5 minutes

For your ship item, answer the three questions above on the combined exercise page, one line each, with a concrete thing or “none”.

Show the model answer

Constructed example: the QA in section 1 saves every run of the check suite, together with the release lead’s approval result, into a dated log. After a few months the log shows which screens often fail. The log is the team’s own data (intangible assets), and once the release lead is used to deciding from the report, dropping it has a cost (switching costs). There is no network effect yet. Write exactly these three lines.

4. Runway (how long it can keep running)

Definition

According to JPMorgan’s guidance, runway is how long a business can keep operating at its current spending before the money runs out: runway equals the cash on hand divided by the net cash burn per period, where net burn equals cash spent minus cash received. The article adds conditions: count only cash and assets that can be turned into cash within about 30 days, exclude funding that is not certain, and the simple calculation is only a rough estimate when spending changes from month to month.

Why it matters

A change can be right and still die because it runs out of what it needs to continue: your hours, the tool quota, your manager’s permission. If you know the limit in advance, you can choose the size of the change.

Apply to the work you are doing

Choose the first blocking resource and apply the formula: the time it can keep running equals the amount remaining divided by the net consumption per week. The resource can be your self-work hours each week (the course’s provisional figure is about 4 hours), the AI tool quota the company grants, or the period the manager allows for a trial. This is an application built by the writer (inference).

Example

The owner has no example yet for this concept in the sense of cash: the OPC article has only one line saying profit and survival come before growth and keep the runway (line 97), without explanation. The nearest thing is a real resource limit: on 11 October the weekly usage reached 75%, so the owner set a rule that the bot only coordinates, writes specs and grades, and heavy work is handed to the CLI (same file, line 68). This is an example of a limit forcing a change of design, not a runway calculation.

Common mistakes

Looking only at the resources you already have and forgetting the resources you do not control, such as the company’s release calendar. Sign: the plan has no blocked days. Fix: ask “what would make me stop that I do not decide?”.

Exercise · 5 minutes

State one blocking resource of the ship item, the amount you have now, the consumption per week, and the result of the division, on the combined exercise page. Where something is an assumption, say so clearly.

Show the model answer

Constructed example (numbers are assumptions): you have 5 hours of self-work each week, of which 1.5 hours are already spent on other things you cannot drop. That leaves 3.5 hours per week for the change. Building the change needs 12 hours, which takes about 3.4 weeks. The course’s build part has only two weeks (Week 3 and Week 4), that is 7 hours in this example, and the course’s provisional build budget is about 8 hours. So 12 hours does not fit. What you do in Week 2: cut the scope, not the quality, until the build estimate is no more than 7 hours; if it cannot be cut, choose the second problem from Week 0 or a smaller internal tool, as the size test in Week 1 already indicated. This finding must happen in Week 2, not in Week 6.
