Learner brief for Week 3

What you will be able to do

After this week, you:

  1. List the questions you need to answer to reach exactly one small decision in the work area of your ship item.
  2. Write a small ontology for that work area: 5 to 10 types of thing, the named relations between them, and the source of each relation, plus 3 to 5 dated decisions.
  3. Package your first skill, which is a bundle of instructions the agent loads when it meets the right kind of task, and let your agent answer three questions about the work area with sources cited.

You will also know one honest fact: no measurement yet shows that an agent with an ontology does better than an agent without one. You must not write “improved by X%” if you have not measured.

What to bring to Session 1

  • The problem frame, the settled number and the spec with acceptance criteria from Week 1 and Week 2.
  • One small decision that repeats in the work area of your ship item, chosen by you in advance.
  • An AI tool of your own choice, and your log of tasks given to AI: what you gave, what the AI returned, how you checked it.
  • You do not need a design system, Figma or special software. You only need a text file.

Self-work after Session 1 (about 6 to 10 hours, estimate not measured)

  1. Finish the ontology: types of thing, relations with sources (where it came from, who confirmed it, whether it is still valid, with a date), dated decisions. Where a relation has no source yet, write “no source yet”.
  2. Package your first skill: one short main file, when to use it, a few dated rules, a pointer to the ontology file. This works with any tool.
  3. Ask your agent three questions about the work area, with the skill. Record the real commands and answers. Optional, if you have time left: ask the same three questions again without the skill to compare; this is not part of the grading criteria.
  4. Let the agent build the first part of your ship item. You verify the result yourself.
  5. Send the record, the screenshot of the ontology and the record of the three questions at least 24 hours before Session 2.

Reading: the learning material for this week, in part D of this page. Do the four exercises on your own ontology (single source of truth, the person who confirms, review date, what you know yourself but have not written down, C1 to C4); they are part of finishing the ontology.

How you are graded

In Session 2, two other learners grade your work, in rotation. They cannot enter your company’s tools, so they grade only from the record, the screenshots and one live run: in your 10-minute “hot seat” turn, they ask your agent three questions about the work area (4 minutes), then ask you Socratic questions (3 minutes); the two graders write down their results on their own, without talking to each other. A spoken account does not count. If the two graders differ, the stricter result is kept.

They answer four questions: is there a record, or only a spoken account; does it link to the number of the ship item; can you answer the Socratic questions about your decisions; does the work clearly separate what is certain from what is only an assumption.

The result is one of three: pass, needs revision, or not enough evidence. You pass when your agent answers with sources cited, and where something does not fit it is marked “not linked yet” instead of guessed. If it needs revision, you revise and submit again.

You also have 5 minutes to defend one important relation in your ontology.

Permitted AI level

  • Break down to the root at the start of Session 1: no AI (AL0). You work on your own page by yourself.
  • Decisions in the ontology: AI may give you a draft for you to challenge (up to AL5). You point out the errors, correct them and defend your choices.
  • Building the ship item and packaging the skill: you may hand the whole task to AI (AL7). You verify the result yourself and keep the log.

If you are stuck

  • You do not know where to start: pick only one decision and four questions, then list the nouns and verbs in them.
  • You do not know who confirms a relation: write “no owner yet” and ask your manager or a colleague. Do not fill it in to make it look complete.
  • Your company does not allow pasting data into outside tools: use a tool the company allows, or build the ontology with the names of the types of thing and no sensitive values.
  • The agent keeps guessing: add the instruction “use only this document, cite the source section, say not linked yet if something is missing”, run it again, and record the real result even if it looks poor.
  • Still stuck after 30 minutes: message the owner with a record of what you tried, before Session 2.

Learning material: ontology, information and skills

Examples labelled “constructed example” were constructed by the author of this material to illustrate. They are not real data and not the owner’s words. The definition used all week is the owner’s: an ontology is the types of thing, the named relations between them, and the source of each relation (the article of 2026-11-11; the course contract records that it is not confirmed whether the article has been published).

Why an ontology matters, beyond design systems

Almost all the owner’s examples sit inside a design system, because that is the owner’s daily work. This part extends to other kinds of work, and says clearly which parts are the owner’s and which are added.

For an individual

The owner argues that a chatbot that looks up documents only needs to find the right passage, while an agent that works every day for one person must make decisions: which variant to choose, where to place it, following which precedent. Finding the right passage is not enough, because the agent also needs to know what that passage connects to, whether it is still valid, and who decided it (the ontology article, section “Quản lý data cho AI đã có từ thời RAG” [Managing data for AI has existed since the RAG era]).

Things that exist only in your head, such as “this kind of bug always goes to Nam”, “this customer never gets the beta version”, do not exist for the agent. The owner quotes the Codex team’s idea: anything the agent cannot read in its context while running does not exist for the agent (same article, section “Phần lớn các khoá học” [Most courses]). The origin of this idea is an OpenAI article; this week uses only the version the owner quotes, and the original article’s exact words could not be verified.

What you lose without it: you must repeat the same background in every turn, and each time you leave out a detail, the agent guesses.

For a business

  • Three agents (UI, research, frontend) read the same definition of “user flow” in one shared file, instead of each place understanding a different meaning (the owner’s Autonomous Design Agent article, section 2.1, according to the concepts report).
  • The project has 325 rulings, meaning recorded decisions of the owner, each with a date and a scope of application; 7 have been replaced and the record says which one replaced them (the ontology article, the table of eight questions, the row “Vì sao Button này lại như thế” [Why this Button is the way it is]). Because of this, a matter decided once does not have to be asked again. These numbers are self-reported in the article; no one has re-run them.

Both examples are from the owner’s anonymised enterprise project. The owner also records the limit: no measurement yet shows that an agent with an ontology does better than an agent without one (the ontology article, section “Giới hạn” [Limits]). This week teaches that limit together with the idea, and does not teach the idea as evidence.

For each kind of work

  • Design. The owner lists five things a designer can do right away without any tool: name things by meaning, design the whole slot, record the reason once as a rule with a date, keep a closed list, answer when the tool asks (the ontology article, section “Designer có thể làm năm việc” [A designer can do five things]).
  • Dev. The OpenAI lesson that the owner quotes is to give the agent a map, not a 1,000-page guide; the instruction file shrinks to about 100 lines acting as a table of contents, pointing into a structured documentation folder. The figure of 100 lines and this lesson are OpenAI’s, not the owner’s.
  • QA and operations. The owner has no example yet for this concept in the form of a named ontology. The closest is the trace chain of acceptance criteria, scenario, evidence with the states CURRENT, STALE, MISSING in the owner’s spec-driven set (the owner does not call it an ontology, and that repository is currently private). For this reason the three examples below are constructed examples.

Building a small ontology with no design system and no Figma

The sourced method: Natalya F. Noy and Deborah L. McGuinness, “Ontology Development 101: A Guide to Creating Your First Ontology”, Stanford, technical report 2001 (read in the session that wrote this material). Their three principles: there is no single correct way to model; work iteratively, a rough first version and then revise; concepts should be close to the nouns and verbs of the domain itself. Their seven steps are: determine the scope, consider reusing existing ontologies, enumerate the terms, define the classes and the hierarchy, define the properties, define the constraints on the properties, create specific instances.

Below is a shortened version for a person with no tools, built from those seven steps and the owner’s three things. This is the author’s way of combining them, not yet run with learners. It needs a text file and one person to try reading it.

  1. Choose one small decision. A decision that repeats in your ship item, for example “classify this bug at which level”.
  2. List the questions that must be answered to reach the right decision, one per line. The owner does this when breaking the task “choose the right Button” into eight questions.
  3. Underline the nouns and verbs. Nouns are candidates for types of thing; verbs are candidates for named relations. At most 5 to 10 types of thing.
  4. Tidy up. Merge words with the same meaning, split words with two meanings, write a one-sentence definition for each type of thing.
  5. Write relations as three-part sentences: “[type A] [relation] [type B]”. Name each relation with a verb.
  6. Write a source for each relation. Three cells: where it came from, who confirmed it, whether it is still valid with a date. A relation with no source yet is marked “no source yet”, not deleted.
  7. Add 3 to 5 dated decisions. Each records the date, the person who decided, the scope, and which old decision it replaces, if any.
  8. Create specific instances. One or two real cases, then go through the ontology: which questions can be answered, which cannot.
  9. Test with an agent. Give the ontology to an agent, ask three questions about the work area, and record the places where it guessed instead of citing a source. Each place it guessed is a hole in the ontology, not a fault of the agent.

A suggested format, usable in any tool, is a text file with three parts: a table of types of thing, a table of relations with sources, and a decision ledger. The owner’s tool, DESIGN:OS Knowledge Builder, is an optional choice for knowledge with verbatim quoted sources. It is not required, and not meant for building an operating ontology.

Constructed example (not real data). A customer-support person, the decision “reply to a plan-change request”. The questions: which plan is the customer on, does the plan allow a change mid-period, who may approve an exception. Nouns: customer, plan, billing period, exception, approver. Verbs: belongs to, allows, approves. The relation “the plan allows a change mid-period” has as its source clause 4 dated 2026-03-01, confirmed by the team lead.

Common mistake. Starting by drawing a beautiful diagram or choosing a tool before listing the questions. The model may be correct for the domain and still not help reach any decision.

Exercise. Do steps 1 to 6 in 30 minutes for one decision of yours, in your ontology file above. Give the file to another person and ask them a question that the ontology must be able to answer. If they cannot find the answer in the file, you are not finished.

Information and knowledge management

The syllabus names four concepts: single source of truth, information ownership, the information lifecycle, and knowledge management. The sources below were found by web search in the session that wrote this material. Where only a secondary summary exists, it is stated clearly.

Single source of truth

Definition. The practice of structuring information models and data schemas so that each data element is created and edited in only one place; other places only refer to it. Source: Wikipedia, “Single source of truth” (read). This is a reference document, not the original article; the original article that named the concept was not found, so the origin is marked not verified. Conditions: copies are still allowed if they are read-only; the concept assumes there is only one truth for each data item, and when sources disagree, the “truth” may only be a record of each party’s claim.

Why it matters. Your ship item depends on numbers and definitions. If two documents say two different things about the same threshold, the agent chooses one and you do not know which.

Apply to running an agent. Each thing in the ontology has exactly one “root” place; other files point back to it and do not copy it. The owner has an example in the same spirit: when two versions of a decision talk about the same matter, the newer one is used and the older one keeps the history, and the owner’s retrieval tool penalises the score of a replaced version (superseded_by, according to the concepts report). This example is about a newer version replacing an older one; it does not speak directly about “a single place”.

Constructed example. The threshold for a “high-severity bug” is written in three places: the wiki, the agent’s instruction file, and a comment in a ticket, with three different numbers. Choose the wiki as the root place, replace the other two with links, and record the date of the change.

Common mistake. Understanding “one source” as “one tool”. The source stresses naming which place holds the approved value, not gathering everything into one system.

Information ownership (data stewardship)

Definition. In the DAMA framework, data governance is the exercise of authority, control and shared decision-making over the management of data assets; data stewardship is the accountability and the responsibility for execution so that data is controlled and used effectively. Source: DAMA International, DAMA-DMBOK: Data Management Body of Knowledge, 2nd edition, chapter 3. Not verified: the year of publication; it is a book; the update page returned a 404 error on 2026-11-13; the book’s introduction page opens and confirms that DAMA International publishes this body of knowledge, but contains no definition. Not verified at the level of the exact words: only a secondary summary could be read, and those summaries differ on whether “owner” and “steward” are two roles or synonyms; the DAMA changelog records that the words owner, custodian, trustee are often synonyms for the person doing the steward work.

Why it matters. A piece of information that no one is responsible for goes out of date and no one corrects it. The agent does not know which information to trust.

Apply to running an agent. Each relation in the ontology has one person who confirms it. In the four layers of things, the owner records that the ontology copies down “who has the permission to decide, meaning what the machine may link by itself and what must be asked of the owner” (the ontology article, section on the four layers). In the spec-driven set, the owner’s answer is recorded with date, email, reason and where it applies (according to the concepts report).

Constructed example. A simple table: the information “refund policy” belongs to the support team lead; “high-severity bug threshold” belongs to the QA team lead; “naming convention” belongs to the dev lead. A fourth column: who may edit without asking.

Common mistake. Writing the name of a whole team as the owner (“QA team”). A team cannot answer the agent’s question at the moment it needs the answer.

Information lifecycle

Definition. Information is created, used and maintained, then kept or removed according to its value. Source: ISO 15489-1:2016, Information and documentation, Records management, Part 1: Concepts and principles. Not verified at the level of the text of the standard: the iso.org page returned a 403 code and only the description and summaries could be read. According to those summaries, the standard does not fix specific retention periods; each organisation sets its own retention schedule.

Why it matters. An ontology without dates makes every rule look as if it were still valid. The agent confidently uses a rule that has expired.

Apply to running an agent. Each rule has a date and a replacement version, the new version wins, the old version keeps the history. The owner does exactly this: rulings have a date and a scope, 7 of the 325 rulings have been replaced and state which one replaced them (the ontology article). The owner also has a 12-hour freshness check gate for the graph data (the Autonomous Design Agent article, section 2.3 step 9, according to the concepts report).

Constructed example. The rule “every high-severity bug must have a screenshot” dated 2026-03-01. On 2026-09-10 it changes to “every high-severity bug must have a screenshot or a record of the commands”. The old line says “replaced by the line dated 2026-09-10”; the agent applies only the new line.

Common mistake. Deleting the old version to keep things tidy. When there is a dispute, no one can trace back why.

Knowledge management

Definition. Nonaka argues that organisational knowledge arises from a continuing dialogue between tacit knowledge (hard to express, held in people) and explicit knowledge (written down); the SECI model has four types of conversion: socialisation, externalisation, combination, internalisation. Sources: Ikujiro Nonaka, “A Dynamic Theory of Organizational Knowledge Creation”, Organization Science 5(1), 1994, 14-37 (on 2026-11-13 the publication details were checked through Crossref and the abstract was read through OpenAlex: the abstract says organisational knowledge is created through a continuing dialogue between tacit and explicit knowledge, and names four types of interaction; the names of the four types and the letters SECI are not in the abstract, and the full text has not been read); Nonaka and Takeuchi, The Knowledge-Creating Company, Oxford University Press, 1995 (publisher not verified). Davenport and Prusak, Working Knowledge: How Organizations Manage What They Know, Harvard Business School Press, 1998, separate data, information and knowledge into three different concepts (the exact words of the definition not verified).

Why it matters. Most of what a good person knows is tacit. The agent can use only what has been written down.

Apply to running an agent. Building the ontology is itself externalisation: turning what you “know yourself” into relations with sources. The owner does not use the SECI framework and has no example yet for this concept under the name of the framework. The closest is the five steps of record, classify, systematise, store, reuse in the Autonomous Design Agent article, section 2.2; that is the author’s inference, not something the owner calls it.

Constructed example. A long-serving QA knows “the payment screen often breaks after a time zone change”. Write it as a relation: “the payment screen is prone to errors when the time zone changes”, source: three bugs in the previous quarter, the person who confirms: that same QA.

Common mistake. Thinking that writing it down is the end. Knowledge that is written down and never reused is not yet an asset; the reuse loop is the last of the owner’s five steps.

They are part of finishing the ontology. Reference: C1, C2, C3, C4.

Packaging your first skill, independent of any tool

Definition. The owner defines a skill as a bundle of instructions that the agent loads when it meets the right kind of task (the ontology article, section “Phần lớn các khoá học” [Most courses]). The standard form in the owner’s toolkit is a short router file with a few detail files loaded as needed; the router is at most 120 lines for a tool-type skill (according to the concepts report). For this week, that standard is only a hint about size.

Why it matters. One huge file that is always loaded makes the agent drift in discipline. The owner’s article writes that if all the rules are copied into the global instruction file, the always-loaded size “will quickly exceed” the 58,000-character limit and the model’s execution discipline declines. That is a consequence the owner predicts in the article, not a measurement and not a law.

How to do it, in any tool.

  1. Create a folder named after the task, for example phan-loai-loi.
  2. Write the main file SKILL.md or an equivalent name: a paragraph “when to use”, three to five rules with dates, and a link to the ontology file.
  3. Put the ontology file (the table of things, the relations with sources, the decision ledger) next to it.
  4. Load it in whatever way your tool allows: the skill folder if the tool has one, otherwise attach or paste the main file at the start of each session and say “use only what is in the document, ask if something is missing”.
  5. Write one line: the agent must cite the name of the source section every time it asserts something, and say “not linked yet” when there is none.

The test with and without the skill (optional, not part of the Week 3 grading criteria). The owner teaches this check: run the same prompt twice, with the skill and without the skill, then count. Context in the OPC article (section pstack): on 28/10 the owner found that Grok Build, the team’s code-writing CLI, ran the command grok -p with no skill at all, so the pstack set of principles had never been applied. The maintainer installed pstack 0.15.36 (58 skills and 2 agents) and ran the same prompt twice: with pstack the bot cited 5 principles by name, without it 0. The owner concluded that installing is not enough, there must be a test proving it has an effect. For your ship item: ask the same three questions twice, and count the number of answers that cite a source. Three questions and two runs are too small a sample to conclude that the skill is “better”; the result is only a note, and it agrees with what the owner admits: no measurement yet proves that an ontology helps the agent do better.

Common mistake. Cramming everything into the skill and always loading it. Solution: keep the main file short like a table of contents, with the detail in other files that are loaded only when needed.

Exercise: package your first skill

Follow the five steps. Record the commands and the real results. Optional, if you have time left: run the test of three questions twice.
